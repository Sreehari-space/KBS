The counter

One hand on the scale. One hand on the phone. A queue behind.

A kirana is the small neighbourhood grocery shop found on every street in India — rice, lentils, oil and soap, most of it weighed out by hand. Its counter is not a desk. Rice is on the scale while the next customer is already reading out their list, the last one is asking to put it on their tab, and the phone doing all of this cost about ₹8,000 and has two bars of signal on a good day.

Nothing in that sentence is a design detail. It is the entire specification. Every decision in this project was made by asking what the counter would do to it.

Network is optional

Not a degraded mode. There is no server, no login and no sync. Offline is the only state the app has ever been in.

Money is the interface

The running total is the largest thing on the screen and never leaves it. Nothing about an amount is ever left to a rounding error.

Two scripts, one counter

Tamil and English, switched from the header because staff change mid-shift — never buried in settings.

A half-finished bill is sacred

The cart auto-saves as it is built. A dropped phone or a dead battery must not cost the shopkeeper the sale in progress.

The billing screen at rest. Sixty-seven Tamil Nadu staples come pre-loaded, so a shop can ring up its first sale before setting anything up.

Scope

Role

Product design, UI system and front-end implementation. Research, interface, design system, and the shipped code are all one piece of work here.

Platform

A web app that installs to the home screen like a native one. Android phones first, then a keyboard-and-mouse counter, then a small receipt printer over Bluetooth.

Stack

React and TypeScript. The data lives in the browser’s own on-device database, and the app’s files are stored on the phone so it opens with no connection. No backend of any kind.

Billing, stock, the customer credit book, returns, end-of-day cash count, barcode scanning, shelf labels, reports and backup.

Principles

Four rules that settled every argument.

The counter sets the clock

If a decision costs the operator a second during a queue, it loses. Animation is capped at 260 ms and can be switched off entirely, because slow motion on a cheap Android is worse than none.

Money is typography, not text

Amounts are set in tabular numerals so columns align, grouped the Indian way, and the paise are stepped down so the rupees read first. The arithmetic behind them never touches a float.

Offline is the normal state

Being offline gets a quiet dot, never a banner. Banners are reserved for things that are actually wrong — storage that will not persist, a backup that has gone stale.

Anything touching money gets tested

Refunds, bill numbering, credit and the payment sheet are pure, tested modules rather than component state. A defect here is not a visual bug; it is a shop losing rupees.

Colour

A teal that survives sunlight, and one mint that never repeats.

Every value below was measured, not eyeballed. A contrast ratio is how far a colour stands out from the one behind it; below about 4.5 : 1, small text gets hard to read.

Two colours were rejected by measurement

A lighter, friendlier teal measured 3.7:1 on white and failed for small text, so the brand hue is darker than it wants to be. The muted grey first drawn at #6B7A77 measured 4.08:1 and was lifted to #5F6E6B for 4.86:1. Neither change was visible in a mockup; both were visible in a contrast check.

The accent is a budget, not a palette

Mint appears once per screen, on the single action that finishes what the operator started — BILL, then Complete sale. Cards are separated by the gap between them rather than by borders or heavy shadows, so the only strong colour on the screen is the thing to press next.

Money

Rupees read first. Paise get out of the way.

₹1,24,560.00

Grouped the Indian way

India groups digits differently: a lakh is a hundred thousand, and the commas fall in different places. A shopkeeper reading 1,24,560 sees the amount at a glance; the same digits written 124,560 have to be counted.

Paise at 82%, dimmed

A paisa is one-hundredth of a rupee, the way a cent is of a dollar. The decimals are set smaller and softer everywhere. They must be there — a bill without them looks rounded off — but they must not compete with the rupees.

Tabular, always

Every numeral is the same width, so a column of amounts lines up on the decimal without a table, and a total that changes does not make the layout twitch.

₹104.00
₹1,160.00
₹52.00
₹9,999.50

And underneath: no floats, ever

Every amount is stored and calculated as a whole number of paise — 62 rupees is held as 6,200, never as 62.00. Computers are famously bad at decimals: ask most software for 0.1 + 0.2 and it answers 0.30000000000000004. That is a curiosity in a spreadsheet and a discrepancy in a shop’s books, the kind the owner has to explain at closing time. Totals, tax, discounts, refunds and change are all settled in whole paise before anything reaches the screen.

Two scripts

Tamil is not a translation layer. It is the default.

The app boots in Tamil. English is the switch, and the switch sits in the header where a second staff member can reach it mid-shift, not three taps deep in settings.

விற்பனை பொருட்கள்

ஆவின் பால் · துவரம் பருப்பு · இட்லி அரிசி

Letter-spacing Squeezing the letters together makes an English headline sit better and makes a Tamil one fall apart, because Tamil letters combine into single shapes. Every heading detects the language, drops the squeeze and gives the line more room.
No uppercasing Tamil has no capital letters. Forcing a label to UPPERCASE does nothing at all in one language and damages the other, so it was removed from every shared component.
Tanglish input Most people type Tamil words using English letters. Typing “ponni arisi” fills in பொன்னி அரிசி by itself, from a word list held on the device and a sound-alike fallback for anything missing. Nobody switches keyboards with a queue waiting.
Headline sizes The same sentence in Tamil runs about a third wider in a taller script, so headings step down a size by language instead of wrapping to five lines on a small phone.

The same screen, the same code. The receipt, the reports and what comes out of the printer are all in Tamil too — not just the buttons around the edge.

The billing screen

Everything within a thumb, and the total never moves.

Search, scan, speak Three ways in, side by side. Typing is fastest for a regular, the camera for anything with a barcode, the mic for hands that are busy with a scoop.
Quick items The shop’s own fastest sellers, named during setup and editable later. Price and stock sit on the face of each tile, so the answer to “how much?” never costs a tap.
The next bill number Shown before the sale as a preview, never reserved. The real number is handed out at the instant the sale is saved — otherwise two fast taps could end up sharing one, and two bills with the same number is a mess nobody can unpick later.
Saved A quiet tick, not a toast. The cart is written to the device as it is built, so a dropped phone reopens on the same half-built bill.
The total bar Pinned to the bottom of the screen where the thumb already is, carrying the largest number in the app and the one mint action beside it.

One sale

Five moves from an empty screen to paper in a customer’s hand.

01 — FIND

Type, scan or speak. Results are ranked so the first one is usually right.

02 — WEIGH

Anything sold by weight asks how much. Packets skip this entirely and go straight into the bill.

03 — CHECK

Lines, quantities and the total. Hold the whole cart if the customer goes back for one more thing.

04 — TAKE

Cash, card, instant bank transfer, or the customer’s credit book — and any split between them. Tap what was handed over, get the change back.

05 — GIVE

Print it, WhatsApp it, or hand the phone over. The bill is already saved either way.

Loose goods

A shop sells rice by the kilo and by the fifty rupees.

Half the sales at a grocery counter are not “one of something”. They are two kilos of rice, or a hundred rupees of groundnut oil — and the customer is as likely to name the money as the weight.

So the quantity sheet works in both directions. By weight, it multiplies up to a price. By amount, it divides down to a weight. The keypad is the same either way, and the shortcuts underneath cover the amounts a grocery shop actually sells: 100 g, 250 g, half a kilo, one, two, five.

The numbers are big because the phone is being tapped at arm’s length with one hand while the other holds the scoop.

Packets don’t ask

A tile priced per piece adds one straight to the cart. Making a packet of biscuits go through a weight sheet would be a tax on the commonest tap in the app.

Stock stays honest

The shelf count comes down in the very same save that records the sale — never in a second step afterwards, which could fail on its own and leave the two disagreeing.

The credit book

Credit is not a way of paying. It is the absence of one.

Getting this wrong cost a week. Getting it right made two other features fall out for free.

Choosing credit names the customer and leaves the balance owing.

What each regular owes, ready for the end of the month.

The modelling mistake that cost a week

Almost every neighbourhood shop runs a running tab for its regulars, settled at the end of the month — khata in India. It was first built as a fourth way of paying, sitting in the row beside cash, card and bank transfer. On screen it looks like one. Underneath it is the opposite: the other three reduce what is owed, and this one is what is owed.

Once that was straightened out, a sale became a total, a list of payments against it, and whatever is left over charged to a named customer. Splitting a bill — ₹200 in cash now, the rest on the book — then needed no extra work at all, and neither did settling up later without disturbing the original bill.

Nothing is erased Every credit given and every rupee collected is a new line in the book. What somebody owes is added up from those lines, so no balance can quietly drift away from its own history.
Bills are never edited A saved bill stays exactly as it was. A mistake becomes a return with its own number, so the day’s takings still add up at closing time.

The bill

One bill. Four ways out of the phone.

The bill on screen is not a picture of a receipt — it is the receipt, torn edges and all, laid out to the exact width of the paper the printer will use. What the shopkeeper checks on glass is what the customer gets in their hand.

Paper A layout the width of the paper roll, sent through the browser’s own print command — for shops with a printer plugged into a computer at the counter.
WhatsApp Plain text, lined up with spaces rather than a table, because that is what survives being pasted into a chat message.
Image The same bill drawn to a canvas, for sharing where text formatting would collapse.
Thermal Straight to a receipt printer over Bluetooth — and sent as a picture rather than as text, because the cheap printers these shops own have no Tamil letters built into them. Send characters and the paper comes out full of empty boxes; send pixels and it prints Tamil.

All four are drawn from one description of the bill. Adding a new way to send it never means working the totals out a second time, and a change to how tax is shown appears in all four at once.

Reports

A number the owner can act on, not a dashboard to admire.

Sales, bills, average bill and credit given — each against the previous period, each opening into the bills behind it.

Busiest hours Sales by hour across the whole period, so a shop can see when to have a second person behind the counter.
Best sellers By revenue, not by count — the ranking that tells the owner what to reorder first.
Profit, honestly Profit is worked out only from items whose buying price has been entered. Treating a missing buying price as zero would flatter the shop and make the figure worthless, so sales without one are shown separately rather than folded in.
Day close Expected cash against counted cash, with the difference stated plainly. The one screen that ends the working day.

Offline

There is no server. That is the feature, not the compromise.

Everything is local

Sales, stock, customers and settings live in the browser’s own database on the phone. The app’s own files are saved there too, so once it is installed it makes no network requests at all — there is nothing left to ask anyone for.

One transaction, or none

Saving a sale writes the bill, the shelf counts, the credit entry and the bill number as one all-or-nothing operation. Either every part of it lands or none of it does. There is no in-between where a sale exists but the stock never moved.

Storage is finite, so say so

A bill takes about a kilobyte. The five megabytes a browser typically allows works out at roughly six weeks for a busy shop — so the app keeps an eye on how much room is left and asks for a backup before it is in trouble, not after.

Trust is shown, not claimed

Being offline gets a small amber dot beside the screen name — it is the normal state here, so it does not get a warning banner. Banners are spent only on things that are genuinely wrong: a browser that might throw the data away, space running out, a backup nobody has taken in a week. Restoring a backup is never silent either; it says what it is about to overwrite, first.

The migration nobody sees

Changing the shape of the database on a device that holds the only copy of a shop’s books is the most dangerous thing this app does. The one such change so far shipped alongside a fix to restoring: the bill numbering is now rebuilt from the restored bills themselves, because an earlier version could hand out the same bill number twice after a restore — and two bills sharing a number is a mess nobody can unpick at closing time.

Dark

Shops open before dawn and close after dark.

Not simply inverted. The brand teal is too dark to read as text on a dark background, so that job is handed to a lighter tone of the same colour.

The receipt stays white in the dark. It is paper — a dark receipt would be a picture of something that cannot exist, and the shopkeeper is checking it against what the printer is about to produce.

First run

The one screen that isn’t a working counter.

A colour per question, heavy display type, and ink drawn by hand. It stops at the front door.

A fresh install used to open on an empty billing screen — technically correct and completely unwelcoming. Three questions later the app carries the shop’s name, knows whether it has to charge sales tax, and has that shop’s own fastest sellers waiting on the front screen.

Setup is the only moment in the product with no queue behind it, so it is the only place that can afford charm over density. The step you are on is the colour of the entire screen, so progress is felt before the rule at the top is read.

Measured, not chosen The black stands out from all three backgrounds by a factor of 13. The same measurement says a white box on the green one stands out by a factor of 1.15 — almost nothing — so the input boxes are marked out by their black outline, never by their fill.
Dark, not dropped The three colours get dark counterparts rather than the whole treatment being dropped, because setting up at night should still feel like this. Every stroke in the drawings takes its colour from the text around it, so the black flips to cream with no second copy of the artwork.

What broke

Five defects that shipped, and what each one actually cost.

A case study without this section is a portfolio. Every one of these was found after it was written, and three of them were losing money.

Credit could not be selected at all

A tidy-up left the credit panel behind a condition that could never be true, so putting a sale on somebody’s tab — and splitting a bill across two ways of paying — silently became impossible. Every automated test still passed, because the logic sat inside the screen itself where no test could reach it. The fix was not the condition; it was pulling every payment decision out into its own tested piece. Putting the original mistake back now fails nine tests.

Returning the same item twice refunded too much

Returning the same item twice was checked against the original sale each time, instead of against everything already sent back. Proven by running it rather than by reasoning about it: ₹50 refunded against a ₹30 sale, and a shelf count going 100 → 102. Returns are now checked against the running total of what has already gone back, they wipe out any credit before handing over cash, and they have their own numbering.

Two bills could share one number after a restore

Restoring a backup brought the bills back but not the count of how many had been issued, so the next sale reused a number. In a shop’s books that is not a bug, it is a mess nobody can unpick. Restoring now works the numbering out from the restored bills themselves, and adds up every customer’s balance again afterwards.

Controls that were invisible — twice

A search box painted the same colour as the page it sat on. Then, after the palette changed, every grey control inside a pop-up panel — because those panels sit on the page colour rather than the card colour. The same mistake twice, and found the same way both times: by looking at the screen, not by reading the change.

A 26-pixel field on the very first screen

During setup, the box for typing an item’s name was too narrow to show its own label or aim a thumb at, because a shared component insisted on a full-width setting that overrode the width its neighbour had asked for. Automated checks passed happily — a 26-pixel box still accepts typing. It was reachable by a script and unusable by a person, and only measuring it caught the difference.

Where it landed

A shop can install it, set it up in four minutes, and bill all day without a network.

What the design work actually was

Less drawing screens than deciding what a busy counter would put up with: how big a number has to be, how few taps a weight can take, which warnings are worth interrupting somebody for, and what happens to a half-finished bill when the phone dies.

What it taught

That a design system is not a colour palette — it is the set of decisions you can still defend a month later. Nineteen of them are written down here with the reasoning attached, including the ones that were wrong.

Still open

Backups are still manual and stay on the phone; an automatic copy kept somewhere else has to land before this holds a real shop’s only books. And the keyboard shortcuts have not yet been watched in the hands of somebody who did not design them.
